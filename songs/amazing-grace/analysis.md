# Amazing Grace: reading

What the song says and how it moves, read before its arrangements are written or revised.

## Text

John Newton, 1779. A testimony in the first person: someone who was lost looks back and is
astonished to have been found. The first verse takes three steps. It begins in wonder
("Amazing grace! how sweet the sound"), names the low point without softening it ("that saved
a wretch like me"), and then turns twice, each time from past to present: "I once was lost, but
now am found, was blind, but now I see."

The weight lies on "I once was lost, but now am found", where the two halves of a life meet in
one line. The tone is gratitude, not triumph; the singer is still surprised. The later verses
go on through fear relieved and dangers passed and end with a look far ahead ("When we've been
there ten thousand years"), so the song grows from verse to verse.

It is sung at thanksgiving, after a confession, at communion, and at funerals.

## Tune

"New Britain", in three-four, with a pickup on the low fifth. The tune is pentatonic: it uses
the degrees 1, 2, 3, 5, and 6 and never the fourth or the seventh. Its range is exactly one
octave, from the low 5 to the high 5.

There are four phrases of four measures. Three of them open with the same gesture, a long 1
and a turn of two eighths up to 3 (`1 . (3 1) | 3 . 2`). The third phrase starts the same turn
from the high 5 instead, the top of the tune, on "I once was lost". Almost every measure is a
long note followed by a short one, which gives the song its rocking, unhurried gait.

The melody waits in two places. In measures 7 and 8 the high 5 on "me!" is held for five
beats over a half cadence; this is the one gap inside the song. In measures 15 and 16 the
final 1 on "see" is held to the end.

## Harmony

Three chords carry most of the song, and they change slowly: G for a measure or two at a
time, C on "sweet" and on "now am", D at the half cadence. That leaves the left hand room to
move. Three chords stand out. G7 on "lost" leans into the C of "now am found". E minor on
"blind" is the only minor chord, on the darkest word. G over D and D7 then lead home on "now
I see".

## Character

Quiet wonder that opens into a plain declaration and returns to stillness. The printed tempo of
84 swings gently, one pulse per measure, and is never heavy.

The climax is the start of the third phrase, "I once was lost", where the tune reaches its top
and the dynamics reach forte. "Was blind" turns inward. "Now I see" ends softly: sight is
given, not won.

## What the arrangements should do

- **Dynamics.** Mezzo-piano at the start, growing through "that saved a wretch like me" to
  forte on "I once was lost", falling back through "found", and piano on "see".
- **The gap on "me!"** (measures 7 and 8) is the hinge of the song: a half cadence that waits
  for the turn. The fill there moves forward and leads up into the high 5 of "I once": a
  walk-up, a seventh that has to resolve, a figure that gathers speed. It must not settle.
- **The last note on "see"** (measures 15 and 16) is the arrival. Whatever moves under it
  comes to rest: an Amen (the four chord over the home bass), a chord that blooms, a line
  that sinks.
- **Alberti bass.** The even eighths suit the rocking gait. Keep the figure low and close;
  let the bass step (D, C, B across the half cadence) so that the figure carries the turn.
- **Gospel.** The song is at home here. Octaves in the bass, the seventh falling under "me!",
  a two-five before the close, and the Amen on "see". Warm, never showy.
- **Jazz.** Shell voicings, soft throughout. The sixth on the home chord keeps the pentatonic
  colour; the two-five chains belong to the cadences, not to the peak.
- **Contemporary.** Suspensions and added seconds fit a tune that has no fourth or seventh to
  rub against. A suspended D under "me!" makes the waiting audible.
- **Majestic.** For a congregation that sings out. Start smaller than the style suggests, so
  that the growth to "I once was lost" is heard, and do not thunder the last line.
- **Around the song.** Introductions set the gait and stay quiet. An Amen is the natural
  ending. A repeat a half step higher suits the later verses, which look ahead.
